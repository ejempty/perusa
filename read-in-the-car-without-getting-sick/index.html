How to read in the car without getting sick (Android passengers)

Updated October 25, 2026 · by Elliott Jempty, developer of the Perusa motion sickness app

Reading in a moving car makes a lot of passengers queasy, and a phone screen is often worse than a paper book. The usual explanation is a mismatch: your inner ear feels the car brake, speed up and turn, while your eyes, fixed on a still page, report that nothing is moving. Nothing below is guaranteed to work for everyone, but each of these may help, and they add up. Start with the ones that cost nothing.

1. Pick the seat that shows you the road

The front passenger seat is the easiest place to read: you see the road ahead, so the car's turns and stops don't take your eyes by surprise. In the back, the middle seat with a view between the front seats is usually easier than a side seat that only shows the side window. Facing forward matters; a seat facing backwards is the hardest place to read.

2. Keep fresh air moving

A warm, stuffy car makes queasiness come on sooner. Open a window a little or point a vent at your face, and keep the cabin on the cool side. Strong smells (food, air fresheners) tend to make it worse.

3. Take breaks before you feel ill, not after

Read in short stretches, then look out at the horizon for a minute or two. Once nausea has started it builds quickly and takes a while to fade, so stopping early is easier than recovering later. If you feel very unwell, put the phone down and look at the road ahead; that helps more than any app.

4. Make the text bigger and hold the phone higher

Larger text means your eyes do less work tracking small lines while the phone moves in your hand. Holding the phone higher, closer to eye level, keeps some of the window in the edge of your vision, so your eyes still see a little of the car's motion. Dark mode at night cuts glare. Scrolling feeds and fast video are usually harder than a steady page of text.

5. Give your eyes the motion: dots that move with the car

Motion cue dots are small dots at the edges of the screen that move with the car's real braking, speeding up and turns. They show your eyes, in your peripheral vision, the motion your body already feels, outside the text you are reading. iPhones have this built in; on Android it is built in only on some of the newest phones (see which Android phones have a setting).

Why slow motion is the part that matters. The international vibration standard ISO 2631-1 puts the motion most linked to sickness at about 0.1 to 0.5 Hz: the slow sway of braking and cornering. Questions

How do I read in the car without getting a headache?

The same things tend to help: bigger text, less glare (dark mode at night, no direct sun on the screen), regular breaks to look into the distance, and fresh air. Headaches that come with nausea in the car are often part of the same motion sickness; if headaches are frequent or severe, talk to a doctor.

Why do I get dizzy reading in the car?

Your balance sense feels the car move while your eyes see a still page, and the brain gets two stories that do not match. Some people feel that as dizziness, some as nausea, some as tiredness or sweating. Giving the eyes some of the motion, by looking out or with motion cues, can reduce the mismatch.

Can you read an e-reader app in the car?

Yes, as long as you are a passenger. A dedicated e-reader with a paper-like screen is easy on the eyes, but it still shows a still page. On a phone or tablet you can add motion cue dots over a reading app; Perusa works over e-reader apps on Android phones.

Is a phone worse than a book?

For many people, yes: a phone is smaller, held lower, scrolls, and has a bright screen. Paper is not immune either. The tips above apply to both.

Can I use Perusa while driving?

No. Perusa is for passengers only, and it asks you to confirm you are a passenger. A driver should be looking at the road, not reading.
