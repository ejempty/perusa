How to not get car sick on a road trip (as the passenger)

Updated October 26, 2026 · by Elliott Jempty, developer of the Perusa motion sickness app

A long drive is hardest on the passenger. The driver sees every turn coming; the person next to them is reading, scrolling, navigating or answering messages, and their inner ear feels braking and curves that their eyes, fixed on a phone, never see. That mismatch is the usual explanation for car sickness. None of the tips below is guaranteed to work for everyone, but each of these may help, and on a road trip they add up over hours.

1. Claim the front seat, facing forward

The front passenger seat is the easiest place to ride: you see the road ahead, so the car's turns and stops don't take your eyes by surprise. If you have to sit in the back, the middle seat with a view between the front seats is usually easier than a side seat. On a long trip, it's fair to ask for the front seat if you're the one who gets queasy.

2. Plan stops before you need them

Nausea builds slowly and fades slowly. A short stop every hour or two, with a few minutes of walking and fresh air, is easier than stopping once you already feel ill. Winding roads and stop-and-go traffic are usually the hardest stretches; that's a good time to put the phone down and look out at the road for a while.

3. Keep the car cool and the air moving

A warm, stuffy car makes queasiness come on sooner. Open a window a little or point a vent at your face. Strong smells (fast food, air fresheners) tend to make it worse for many people. Light snacks and water are easier on a long drive than a heavy meal right before setting off.

4. Choose easier things to do in the passenger seat

Some things are easier on a car-sick passenger than others. Audio (podcasts, audiobooks, a shared playlist), conversation and looking out are the easiest. Steady reading with big text is next. Fast scrolling feeds, small text and busy games are usually the hardest. Download maps, music and books before you leave, so you're not staring at a loading screen on a bumpy road.

5. If you want your phone: bigger text, phone higher, and dots that move with the car

Make the text bigger and hold the phone closer to eye level, so a little of the window stays in the edge of your vision. Dark mode cuts glare at night.

Motion cue dots are small dots at the edges of the screen that move with the car's real braking, speeding up and turns. They show your eyes, in your peripheral vision, the motion your body already feels. iPhones have this built in; on Android it is built in only on some of the newest phones (see which Android phones have a setting).

A road trip passenger checklist. Front seat if you can get it. Stops every hour or two. Window cracked or a vent on your face. Water and a light snack. Maps, music and books downloaded before you go. A phone charger. And, if you'll be on your phone, something to show your eyes the car's motion.

Questions

Why do I get car sick as a passenger but not when I drive?

The driver steers, brakes and watches the road, so their eyes and body agree about what the car is doing and they can predict every move. A passenger, especially one looking down at a phone, feels the motion without seeing it coming. Many people who never feel sick driving do feel sick riding.

Why is the back seat worse?

From the back you see less of the road ahead and more of the side windows, so turns and stops come as more of a surprise. Facing forward with a view between the front seats helps.

Does it help in a rental car?

The same tips apply in any car. Perusa needs nothing from the car itself: it runs on your Android phone, so it works the same in a rental, a friend's car or a rideshare.

What about car-sick kids?

Perusa is made for adult passengers on their own phones; it isn't designed for children, and most kids' tablets have no GPS, which Perusa needs to tell that the car is moving. For a child who gets car sick, the seat, air and stop tips above may help; ask their doctor about anything more.

Can the driver use Perusa?

No. Perusa is for passengers only, and it asks you to confirm you are a passenger. A driver should be looking at the road.
