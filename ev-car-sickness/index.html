Why passengers feel car sick in EVs (regen braking) and what helps

Updated October 25, 2026 · by Elliott Jempty, developer of the Perusa motion sickness app

A common complaint from people who ride in electric cars: "I never got car sick until we bought an EV." It is reported often enough that researchers have started to look at it, though the evidence is still early. Nobody has a single proven cause, but three things about how EVs drive fit the usual explanation of car sickness, a mismatch between what your inner ear feels and what your eyes see.

1. Regenerative braking slows the car all the time

In most EVs, lifting off the accelerator makes the motor slow the car down to recover energy. Many cars let the driver set this strongly enough to drive with one pedal. For a passenger, that means far more small decelerations than in a petrol car: every time the driver eases off, the car slows. That slow push forward and back, repeated over and over, sits in the band of motion most linked to sickness, about 0.1 to 0.5 Hz in the international vibration standard ISO 2631-1.

2. A quiet cabin removes a warning

In a petrol car, the engine note tells you, without thinking about it, that the car is about to speed up or slow down. An EV is nearly silent, so the change in speed is the first you know of it. The less warning your senses get, the bigger the mismatch tends to feel.

3. Quick, smooth acceleration

Electric motors deliver strong acceleration instantly. Smooth does not mean gentle: a passenger reading a phone feels the push without seeing it coming.

What the driver can try

  • Turn regenerative braking down, or choose a mode where the car coasts more when you lift off, if your car offers one. The names and options differ by make and model (Tesla, like most EVs, has its own settings for how the car slows and stops); the owner's manual lists them.
  • Ease off the accelerator gradually instead of lifting all at once, so each slow-down builds up gently.
  • Accelerate gently with passengers on board, especially from a stop.
  • Choose a comfort or chill drive mode if the car has one.

What the passenger can try

  • Sit in front or where you can see the road ahead, and look up often.
  • Keep air moving; a warm, still cabin makes it worse.
  • Take breaks from the screen before you feel ill.
  • Give your eyes the motion. Motion cue dots at the edge of the screen move with the car's braking, speeding up and turns, so your eyes see what your body feels. More ways to read in the car without getting sick.

Questions

Do electric cars cause more motion sickness?

Many passengers say so, and the way EVs slow down and speed up gives a plausible reason. Research on it is still early, and plenty of people ride in EVs without any trouble.

Does one-pedal driving make passengers car sick?

It can for some people, because the car slows every time the driver lifts off. A lower regen setting, or easing off the pedal gradually, often makes the ride feel smoother for passengers.

Is it worse in the back seat?

Usually, because you see less of the road ahead. The middle rear seat with a view forward is usually easier than a side seat.
